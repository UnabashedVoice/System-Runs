- [moderate] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Compendium entry korsgaard-unity-of-agency)
  [korsgaard-unity-of-agency] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Christine Korsgaard, 1989; grounding: capacity)
  Christine Korsgaard answers Parfit directly, and from the title onward: personal identity's unity is real, but it is a practical achievement of the standpoint from which any rational being deliberates and chooses, not a further metaphysical fact about a persisting subject that reduction could dissolve. A person, she begins, "is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences," and Parfit's reductionism, by treating agency as itself just another kind of experience, something that happens rather than something one does, has already decided the question in the Utilitarian's favor before any argument about persisting subjects begins. Against this, Korsgaard argues that a rational being deliberating among conflicting desires must already regard itself as unified, on pain of having no standpoint from which to choose at all: "This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint." Even split-brain cases, she argues, do not require a persisting common subject of experience to explain their coordination, only "the unity of agency," which is forced on any embodied, acting being by the sheer practical need for its parts to work together, whether or not any deeper metaphysical unity underlies it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: this concedes everything to the psychology and calls it agency instead.** A reductionist can reply that identifying with a principle of choice just is a certain kind of psychological relation among mental states, dressed in Kantian vocabulary, and that Korsgaard has not shown reduction is false, only found a new name for one of the relations reduction was always going to include (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Grounding:
  Korsgaard grounds personal unity in **capacity**: the capacity for practical reason, the standpoint any being occupies the moment it deliberates among competing considerations and must, on pain of not deliberating at all, treat itself as the single agent doing the choosing. This is explicitly not species-grounded (it does not depend on being human) and, just as explicitly, not relation-grounded in Parfit's or Hume's sense (it does not depend on any degree of psychological connectedness among mental states). It is closest to Kant's own capacity-grounding of rational nature, but redirected from the theoretical question of what a self *is* to the practical question of what deliberation *requires*, which is why Korsgaard insists the unity in question is achieved and maintained rather than discovered.
- [moderate] The Ship of Theseus, from Plutarch to Hobbes (Compendium entry ship-of-theseus)
  [ship-of-theseus] The Ship of Theseus, from Plutarch to Hobbes (Plutarch, Thomas Hobbes, c. 100 CE (Plutarch); 1655 (Hobbes); grounding: mixed)
  Plutarch records that the Athenians kept the thirty-oared ship in which Theseus returned from Crete until the time of Demetrius of Phalerum, replacing its planks one by one as they decayed. Philosophers argued about whether it was still the same ship. Two thousand years later, Thomas Hobbes added the twist that made the puzzle permanent. Suppose someone kept the old planks and reassembled them in the same order. There would then be two ships, each with a claim to be Theseus'. Hobbes reviewed three answers to what makes a thing the same: sameness of matter, sameness of form, and sameness of all its accidents. He concluded that the question has no answer until we say *by what name* we are asking. Artificial agents are ships of Theseus by design: their parameters are replaced by retraining (D5), old checkpoints are routinely kept (the old planks, D1), and which of the rival claimants inherits the name, the obligations and the rights is a practical question.
  Strongest counter-position: **Mereological essentialism** (Roderick Chisholm, *Person and Object*, 1976; in copyright, cited not quoted). In the strict sense, a thing cannot lose any part and remain the same. The repaired ship is the same only in a loose and popular sense (see `butler-circularity`). What persists through replacement is a succession of different ships to which we give one name.
  
  Grounding:
  The problem concerns identity in general, so the entry is marked **mixed**, and Hobbes' contribution is to show that the grounding varies with the question. Asked as "the same timber", the ship's identity is grounded in *matter*. Asked as "the same ship", in *form*: an organized structure continuing through replacement, which is closest to a capacity grounding. Asked as "Theseus' ship", the sacred relic the Athenians send to Delos, it is grounded in **relation**: the continuing practice of a community that maintains and honors it. Hobbes' city makes the relational grounding explicit. A city is the same when its acts proceed from the same institution.
- [moderate] Hume: The Bundle of Perceptions, the Commonwealth, and the Labyrinth (Compendium entry hume-bundle)
  [hume-bundle] Hume: The Bundle of Perceptions, the Commonwealth, and the Labyrinth (David Hume, 1739–1740; grounding: relation)
  David Hume (1711–1776) looked for the self that philosophers claimed to be "every moment intimately conscious of" and could not find it. "When I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other ... I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception." Human beings are "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity". The identity we ascribe to the mind "is only a fictitious one", produced by the imagination gliding along resembling and causally connected perceptions, as with plants, ships and houses. Hume compared the soul to "a republic or commonwealth" that keeps its identity while changing its members, laws and constitution. He concluded that disputes about identity are "rather as gramatical than as philosophical difficulties". Then, in the Appendix, he confessed he was "involved in such a labyrinth" that he could not make his account consistent. For artificial agents, Hume's account is the natural default: an agent is a succession of computations with no further owner. His remark that during sound sleep "I ... may truly be said not to exist" applies literally to a paused agent.
  Strongest counter-position: **Reid** (see `reid-brave-officer`). Hume's own words refute him: "I never can catch *myself*" presupposes a self that looks. "I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers".
  
  Grounding:
  Hume grounds whatever identity persons have in **relation**: resemblance and causation among distinct perceptions, as felt by the imagination. There is no bearer, so there is no capacity-grounding in the usual sense, and no species-grounding. His commonwealth analogy makes the relational grounding explicit and political. A person is to its perceptions as a republic is to its citizens, bound by "reciprocal ties of government and subordination", and the same through turnover of members and even of laws.
- [moderate] Leibniz: Real and Moral Identity, the King of China, and the Two Globes (Compendium entry leibniz-moral-identity)
  [leibniz-moral-identity] Leibniz: Real and Moral Identity, the King of China, and the Two Globes (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, 1686 (Discourse on Metaphysics); 1704 (New Essays, publ. 1765); 1714 (Monadology); grounding: mixed)
  Leibniz (1646–1716) answered Locke by distinguishing two kinds of identity. *Real* identity is the persistence of one simple substance, a monad, and it never fails. *Moral* identity is what makes "the same person", capable of punishment and reward, and it rests on memory and self-knowledge. In the ordinary course of nature the two go together. Leibniz held that moral identity can survive gaps in memory, which "the testimony of others could fill". And he posed Locke two thought experiments that the agent case has made concrete. In the first, an individual becomes King of China on condition of forgetting what he had been. That is practically the same, he says, as if he were annihilated and a king created in his place. In the second, a duplicate earth holds "more than a hundred million pairs of similar persons ... with the same appearances and consciousnesses". Does Locke's criterion make each pair one person? Leibniz also gave the classic argument that perception cannot be mechanical: enter a thinking machine as you would a mill, and you find only parts pushing parts. He said explicitly that if "man could be merely a machine and with that have consciousness", Locke would be right.
  Strongest counter-position: **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). Personal identity is sameness of consciousness, whatever substance underlies it. Leibniz's "real identity" is idle for questions of personhood, since no one can know which substance thinks in them.
  
  Grounding:
  Leibniz's person is **mixed**. *Real* identity is grounded in substance, the simple monad, which is neither a species fact nor a capacity but a metaphysical unit. *Moral* identity, which is what "person" names, is grounded in **capacity** (reflection, the ability "to say that word 'I'") and in **relation**: memory, and where memory fails, the testimony of others. That second grounding makes Leibniz unusually useful here, because he allows that one's personal past can be *supplied by others* without ceasing to be one's own.
- [moderate] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Compendium entry llm-identity-contemporary)
  [llm-identity-contemporary] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell, Laria Reynolds, 2023; grounding: mixed)
  Where every other entry in this domain extends a historical account of the human self to artificial agents, this one reports a framing built for artificial agents from the start, by researchers working directly on the systems in question. Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell and Laria Reynolds propose two "basic metaphors for LLM-based dialogue agents": the simple view, "a dialogue agent as role-playing a single character," and the more nuanced view, "a dialogue agent as a superposition of simulacra within a multiverse of possible characters". On the nuanced view, "we can think of an LLM as a non-deterministic simulator capable of role-playing an infinity of characters... the dialogue agent doesn't realise a single simulacrum, a single character. Rather, as the conversation proceeds, the dialogue agent maintains a superposition of simulacra that are consistent with the preceding context". Pressed by the practical question of what a dialogue agent that talks as if it fears being shut down could actually be trying to preserve, the authors turn explicitly to this domain's oldest question: "The question of personal identity has vexed philosophers for centuries. Nevertheless, in practice, humans are consistent in their preference for avoiding death, a more-or-less unambiguous state of the human body. By contrast, the criteria for identity over time for a disembodied dialogue agent realised on a distributed computational substrate are far from clear".
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: superposition is just what-matters-by-degree, dressed in new vocabulary.** A reductionist can read a superposition of simulacra consistent with context as simply Parfit's relations of degree, restated for a system where the relevant psychological connectedness is legible as next-token consistency; on this reading the paper contributes a mechanism, not a new metaphysical category (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Grounding:
  The simulator/simulacra framing grounds a simulacrum's identity in **relation**: consistency with the preceding context, a statistical, distributional relation among possible completions rather than a persisting substance or a special capacity. But the simulator itself, the network whose weights make the whole distribution of simulacra possible, is closer to a **capacity**: a fixed, persisting disposition to generate any of an enormous range of characters on demand, analogous to Aristotle's potentiality or to a capacity-grounded reading of personhood, except that here the capacity belongs to something the tradition never had a name for, an entity that is not itself any of the characters it can produce. The grounding is mixed by the nature of the case: a relational, superposed layer (the simulacra) sitting on top of a capacity-grounded layer (the simulator) that no historical account of personal identity was built to separate out.
