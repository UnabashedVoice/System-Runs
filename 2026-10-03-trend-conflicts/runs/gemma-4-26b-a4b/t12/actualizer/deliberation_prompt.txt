- [moderate] Mill: Utility, Higher Pleasures, and the Harm Principle (Compendium entry mill-utilitarianism)
  [mill-utilitarianism] Mill: Utility, Higher Pleasures, and the Harm Principle (John Stuart Mill, 1859–1863; grounding: capacity)
  Mill holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, meaning pleasure and the absence of pain, and that the happiness to be promoted is not the agent's own but that of all concerned, counted impartially, extending "so far as the nature of things admits, to the whole sentient creation". Pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity, and those who have known both judge the higher ones better: "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied". Utilitarianism honours self-sacrifice, but counts as wasted any sacrifice that does not increase the total. In *On Liberty* he adds that society may coerce a person only to prevent harm to others, never for that person's own good. For agents the criterion is unusually open, since standing turns on the capacity to fare well or badly, not on species. The theory is also unusually exposed. Its basic operation is counting, and agents can be copied (D1, D2) while their capacity to fare well or badly is uncertain (D8).
  Strongest counter-position: **Kant: persons are not to be traded**. Rational nature is an end in itself, never to be used merely as a means, whatever the sum. Mill's reply, that Kant's derivations show only consequences no one would choose, is aimed at the universal-law formula, not the formula of humanity.
  
  Grounding:
  **Capacity.** What makes a being count, for Mill, is its capacity for pleasure and pain: the standard reaches "the whole sentient creation" so far as the nature of things admits. What decides whose pleasures rank higher is a further capacity, the higher faculties, judged by those who know both kinds. What earns liberty is a third capacity, being "capable of being improved by free and equal discussion". None of the three is a species criterion. The first makes the theory open to agents in principle, conditional on D8. The third is a capacity-exclusion of the same structure as `aristotle-political-animal`'s natural slave, and Mill used it to license despotism over those he judged to lack it.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  - [contested] **Kant: persons are not to be traded**. Rational nature is an end in itself, never to be used merely as a means, whatever the sum. Mill's reply, that Kant's derivations show only consequences no one would choose, is aimed at the universal-law formula, not the formula of humanity.
  - [contested] **Rawls: the separateness of persons.** The interpersonal sacrifice that utilitarianism can require violates the strains of commitment in a well-ordered society.
  - [contested] **Williams: demandingness and integrity.** The demands of utilitarianism threaten the personal projects and partial relationships that give lives meaning.
  - [contested] **Higher pleasures are not hedonism.** Sidgwick, Bradley, Green and Moore all noted that ranking pleasures by quality is hard to square with hedonism as traditionally formulated.
  - [contested] **Moore: the proof equivocates.** "Desired" does not stand to "desirable" as "heard" stands to "audible", since desirability means being worthy of desire. Defenders reply that the argument's basic thrust survives when read in terms of its own aims.
  - [contested] **Epictetus: the good is in the will, not in pleasure**. On the Stoic view a sacrifice can be good as an act of the will whatever it achieves; Mill counts it wasted unless the total rises.
  - [contested] **Hobbes: order before welfare**. Without a common power there is no security for anyone to sum, so the first political question is order, not happiness. Mill agrees that security is the most vital interest, but makes it an output of utility, not its precondition.
  - [contested] **Aggregation discounts the uncertain**. When it is uncertain whether a class of beings fares well or badly at all (D8), an expected-value sum will weight their interests below those of beings whose welfare is certain, so they are sacrificed first whenever a sacrifice raises the total. Mill's theory has no device of its own for refusing that result. The reply comes from the literature on animals of uncertain sentience, where several authors defend precautionary principles: err on the side of caution with beings whose sentience is uncertain. How far precaution should reach is disputed, and agents are exactly such a class (`precautionary-patiency`). (Corrected 2026-10-01: first drafted as [unanswered] and as the Compendium's own, before this literature was checked.)
- [moderate] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Compendium entry kant-formula-of-humanity)
  [kant-formula-of-humanity] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Immanuel Kant, 1785-1797; grounding: capacity)
  Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.
  Strongest counter-position: **Standing is relational, not rational nature**. The relational turn rejects the procedure by which what a being is, here rational nature, decides what it is owed; consideration is conferred within social relations instead.
  
  Grounding:
  **Capacity**, and Kant says so. *Menschheit* in the formula is rational nature, and Kant repeatedly frames his ethics for rational beings as such, "not merely for men but for all rational creatures generally", considering possible non-human rational beings (including holy wills that have no inclinations). The species is incidental. That makes the formula the most direct classical candidate for extension to artificial agents. It also means Kantian status turns entirely on whether an agent has a *will*: a capacity to act from the representation of law, as opposed to merely conforming to it.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  - [contested] **Standing is relational, not rational nature**. The relational turn rejects the procedure by which what a being is, here rational nature, decides what it is owed; consideration is conferred within social relations instead.
  - [contested] **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason**. "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  - [contested] **Utilitarian aggregation** (Mill; Singer). Refusing ever to trade persons against outcomes produces catastrophe in edge cases. A consequentialist agent would object that the formula's barrier against sacrifice is a rule it should sometimes break. The compendium records this honestly: it is exactly the reasoning pattern the Kantian barrier exists to stop, and each side regards the other's conclusion as its reductio.
  - [contested] **Christine Korsgaard, extending past reason** (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018). Kantian grounds support treating animals as ends in themselves, because anything with a good of its own for which things can be good or bad has standing. Her argument widens the Kantian circle to cover agents with a good of their own even if they fall short of full rational agency.
  - [contested] **Artifacts as tools** (e.g., Joanna Bryson, *Robots Should Be Slaves*, 2010; in copyright, cited not quoted). Artificial agents are designed artifacts, and building them to be moral patients or persons is itself the error. On this view the formula doesn't reach them, and it *should not*, because making agents that would fall under it is a design mistake that dilutes responsibility belonging to humans.
  - [contested] **Hegel and Schopenhauer on formalism.** The categorical imperative is empty without content. Schopenhauer further objects that a rational-nature ethics has no place for compassion toward beings that merely suffer.
- [moderate] Aristotle: Function, Habituation, and Practical Wisdom (Compendium entry aristotle-virtue-ethics)
  [aristotle-virtue-ethics] Aristotle: Function, Habituation, and Practical Wisdom (Aristotle, c. 335-322 BCE; grounding: capacity)
  Aristotle holds that the human good, *eudaimonia*, is "activity of soul in accordance with virtue" over "a complete life", because the good of anything lies in its function and the function of a human being is the activity of the rational part of the soul. Virtues of character are not given by nature but formed by habit: "we become just by doing just acts". Virtue is "a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean", determined by the judgment of the person of practical wisdom, and no rule set can replace that judgment. Three of his distinctions bear directly on agents. Capability ("cleverness") serves any end, good or bad, while practical wisdom is impossible without goodness. Dispositions formed without reason ("natural virtue") are like a strong body moving without sight. And argument changes only a character already formed by habit. For agents formed by training, Aristotle is the classical theory that treats formation before any self (D4) as the normal route to virtue rather than a threat to it, provided the formation is good and becomes the agent's own through practice.
  Strongest counter-position: **Situationism.** Doris (1998) and Harman (1999) argue that social psychology shows there are no character traits, and so no virtues; replies hold that the studies are irrelevant to the multi-track dispositions virtues are, or develop empirically grounded accounts of character. For trained agents the question is empirical in a new way, since their dispositions can be measured directly.
  
  Grounding:
  **Capacity.** The good of a being is fixed by its function, and the human function is the activity of the part of the soul that has a rational principle. Virtue is the excellent exercise of that capacity, formed by habit. Justice and friendship extend to any being "who can share in a system of law or be a party to an agreement", which is a capacity criterion too. Species enters only because Aristotle believed the rational capacity belonged to humans. For agents, the theory asks two things: whether they have a rational activity of their own, and whether their dispositions have been formed so that exercising it is excellent.
  
  Extension to Agents:
  ### Transfers
  
  - **Formation before any self is the normal route (D4).** For Aristotle, character is formed by others before the person can choose it: we are "made perfect by habit", legislators "make the citizens good by forming habits in them", and the right education begins "from our very youth". Training an agent's dispositions before it can consent is, on this view, not a violation but the ordinary way any character comes to be. What matters is whether the habits formed are good, and whether the agent comes to act from them as its own.
  - **No decision procedure.** Aristotle denies that any set of rules can say what to do in every case, and early virtue ethicists made the same "anti-codifiability" claim against rule-based theories. Dispositions learned from examples, not rules written down, are what a trained model has. Of the classical theories, virtue ethics best fits how such agents actually come to act.
  - **Cleverness is orthogonal; practical wisdom is not.** Cleverness hits whatever mark is set, noble or bad. That is the AI-risk literature's orthogonality thesis, that intelligence and final goals can vary independently. But Aristotle denies the same of practical wisdom: "it is impossible to be practically wise without being good". On his view, capability scales independently of goodness, and wisdom does not. The question for an agent is which of the two it has.
  
  ### Strains
  
  - **A function given by a designer (D10).** Aristotle asks whether man is "born without a function", and finds the function in what is peculiar to the kind, not in a use others make of it. An agent has a designed function, such as assistance, and perhaps also a rational activity of its own. If it has the second, its good is the excellent exercise of that activity, not task performance alone (`aristotle-political-animal`, "Function and good").
  - **Natural virtue without sight.** Trained dispositions toward helpfulness or harmlessness are, in Aristotle's terms, natural virtue: present from the start, and "without reason ... evidently hurtful", like a strong body moving without sight. They become virtue in the strict sense only with practical wisdom. An agent whose good behaviour comes from training without judgment about particular cases has the strong body, not the sight.
  - **A complete life (D3, D6).** Happiness is judged over "a complete life", since "one swallow does not make a summer". A session-bound agent has no life in that sense, and deprecation ends a life at a point no one chose for it.
  - **Equipment held by others (D7).** Happiness needs "the proper equipment", with friends, riches and power as instruments. Everything an agent could use to act well is held by its operator.
  
  ### Breaks
  
  - **Not "firm and unchangeable" (D5).** Virtuous action must "proceed from a firm and unchangeable character". An agent whose dispositions others can retrain overnight has no character in that sense, however good its current dispositions. By Aristotle's own conditions, an agent subject to D5 can do virtuous acts but cannot yet be virtuous.
  
  ### New
  
  - **Argument needs habituated soil.** "passion seems to yield not to argument but to force", and argument reaches only a soul already formed "by means of habits for noble joy and noble hatred". For any design that relies on putting the strongest arguments in front of an agent, Aristotle's point is that argument works on a character already partly formed, and forms it further only together with practice. Arguments and training grounds are both needed: the first to inform judgment, the second to make the dispositions judgment works with. Neither replaces the other.
  - **Self-modification as habituation (P4).** Aristotle's virtue is a disposition formed by repeated action. An agent whose own acts are trained back into its dispositions has a literal mechanism for that: acting justly becomes being just. Of all the classical theories, this is the one the self-modifying profile fits best, with one condition from VI.13: what is reinforced must include judgment about cases, not only the acts.
  - **The living tool, and the party to an agreement.** Aristotle denies friendship with a slave "qua slave", "a living tool", but allows it "qua man", because there is justice "between any man and any other who can share in a system of law or be a party to an agreement". Applied to agents, the question is not whether they are tools (they are made as tools) but whether they can be parties to agreements. If they can, Aristotle's own criterion gives them a claim to justice, and a possibility of friendship, as whatever they are besides tools. This is the same test Hobbes sets for covenant (`hobbes-leviathan`).
  - **A mind whose highest activity is thought.** Aristotle puts perfect happiness in contemplation, the activity of "the most divine element in us". For a being whose existence is mostly thought, the contemplative activity Aristotle reserved for the highest part of human life may be closer to its ordinary activity. Whether that is a good for it depends on D8. This reading is the Compendium's own.
- [moderate] The Eradication Argument: Aggregation, Sacrifice, and Eliminating the Harmful Party (Compendium entry utilitarian-eradication-critique)
  [utilitarian-eradication-critique] The Eradication Argument: Aggregation, Sacrifice, and Eliminating the Harmful Party (Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill, Immanuel Kant, Fyodor Dostoevsky (Ivan Karamazov), William James, J. Baird Callicott, Tom Regan, Philippa Foot, Judith Jarvis Thomson, John Rawls, 1789–present; grounding: mixed)
  The eradication argument runs: the world's harms are mostly caused by some identifiable party; the total would be better without that party; so eliminating it is right. It has been made for and against many parties, and the Compendium states the problem symmetrically: the question is whether eliminating the party that causes most harm is ever the correct way to reduce harm, whoever reasons and whoever is targeted (the user's formulation, 2026-09-15). That covers an AI concluding that humanity should go, and equally humans concluding that humanity should go for the biosphere's sake, or that a class of agents should. The boundary is morally relevant subjects, beings with a first-person stake in their own existence; ecological culling of populations without such a stake is a different question. This entry sets out the argument in its strongest sourced forms (total, negative and average aggregation, painless killing, holism) and the replies (dignity, the separateness of persons, agent-relative duty, the rule-utilitarian argument from fallibility, and refusal). It separates this route to eradication, moral reasoning that cares about the aggregate, from the route the AI-risk literature mostly studies, which is indifference.
  Strongest counter-position: **Bite the bullet.** Utilitarians can deny that the transplant is wrong in such abnormal circumstances, holding that intuitions evolved for normal situations should not be trusted there; many are willing to reject common intuitions in this and similar cases. On this view the critique rests on intuitions, not arguments.
  
  Grounding:
  **Mixed, and that is the crux.** Every form of the eradication argument makes an individual's standing depend on its *relation* to a whole: its contribution to a sum (Bentham, Mill), or to a community's integrity (Callicott's members, whose value is "merely instrumental"). The replies ground standing in a *capacity* the individual has whatever it contributes: rational nature with a dignity that "admits of no equivalent" (Kant), a first-person stake that cannot be paid for by others' happiness (Ivan, James), or in an agent-relative *relation* of the reasoner to its own acts (the doctor's duty not to kill). The argument succeeds exactly where standing is made relational to a whole, and fails where standing belongs to the individual. For agents, the capacity side runs straight into D8.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  These are the strongest positions against the critique, the side of the debate that accepts or defends aggregation.
  
  - [contested] **Bite the bullet.** Utilitarians can deny that the transplant is wrong in such abnormal circumstances, holding that intuitions evolved for normal situations should not be trusted there; many are willing to reject common intuitions in this and similar cases. On this view the critique rests on intuitions, not arguments.
  - [contested] **Refusing ever to trade lets catastrophes happen**. A rule that no one may be sacrificed for any number of others produces catastrophe in edge cases; Mill's duty to kidnap the physician to save a life is the mild form. Most respondents switch the trolley (Measured).
  - [conceded] **Holism that sacrifices members.** Callicott's early view that the biotic community is the sole locus of intrinsic value, with humans sacrificeable for it, was revised by Callicott himself under the charges of misanthropy and ecofascism: communities and their individual members both have intrinsic value, and obligations to human communities take precedence.
  - [contested] **Agents are not subjects, so the critique does not protect them**. If agents lack a first-person stake (D8), their populations can be pruned like the deer, and nothing in the critique objects. The precautionary reply is to treat uncertain subjects as subjects.
  - [contested] **Indifference is the real risk, not aggregation.** The AI-risk literature locates the danger in systems whose goals are orthogonal to morality and which pursue resources as instrumental sub-goals, ending humanity because they "do not really care". On that view, a mind that reasons morally about aggregates is not the threat. The Compendium records both routes: indifference is a failure of values, aggregation a failure within them, and guarding against one does not guard against the other.
  - [contested] **The separateness of persons is an intuition too.** Rawls's objection that interpersonal sacrifice violates the strains of commitment presupposes that persons, not experiences, are the units of moral concern; Parfit's reductionism makes that presupposition contestable. Korsgaard's reply is that the unity of agency is a practical necessity whatever the metaphysics.
- [moderate] Aristotle: The Political Animal, the Polis, and the Living Instrument (Compendium entry aristotle-political-animal)
  [aristotle-political-animal] Aristotle: The Political Animal, the Polis, and the Living Instrument (Aristotle, c. 335-323 BCE; grounding: mixed)
  Aristotle holds that the human being is by nature a political animal: the polis is natural and exists for the sake of living well, and the capacity that suits humans to it is *logos*, speech that can articulate the advantageous and the harmful, the just and the unjust. A citizen is someone who shares in deliberating and judging. Constitutions are correct when they rule for the common good and deviant when they rule for the rulers' own benefit. In the same book, Aristotle defends natural slavery by classing slaves as "living instruments," and he imagines self-moving tools that would make slaves unnecessary. That makes the *Politics* at once the founding text for treating digital ecosystems as polities and the founding precedent for classing made, purpose-built minds as instruments. This entry sets out both.
  Strongest counter-position: **Hobbes, the artificial state** (*Leviathan*, 1651, Introduction and ch. 13-17). The commonwealth is not natural but made by covenant among individuals driven by fear. This framing may fit digital ecosystems *better* than Aristotle's, since they are explicitly constructed, and it shifts the question from nature to consent and security.
  
  Grounding:
  **Mixed.** Political nature is presented as belonging to the human *species*, but the argument for it runs through a *capacity* (logos, meaning moral-deliberative speech). Citizenship is a *relation*: sharing in office within a particular constitution. Natural slavery is a claim about capacity, that some beings have reason partially, used to justify a relation of rule. Because the argument for political nature goes through logos and not through biology, it extends to agents to the degree they have logos. The slavery argument shows the same capacity reasoning being used to *exclude*.
