- [moderate] Boethius: Person as 'Individual Substance of a Rational Nature', and the Mask (Compendium entry boethius-person-definition)
  [boethius-person-definition] Boethius: Person as 'Individual Substance of a Rational Nature', and the Mask (Boethius (Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius), Richard of St Victor (as critic), c. 512; grounding: capacity)
  In a short theological treatise written around 512, Boethius gave the Latin West its definition of a person: "the individual substance of a rational nature" (*naturae rationabilis individua substantia*). Each word excludes something. *Substance* excludes accidents and qualities. *Individual* excludes universals: "man" is not a person, but Plato is. *Rational nature* excludes stones, trees and horses: "no one ever said that a stone had a person". The definition was adopted by Aquinas and governed Western thinking about persons for a thousand years, and it still shapes legal and theological use. Boethius also recorded where the word came from. *Persona* was the actor's mask, through which the voice sounded (*per-sonare*), and which marked the character being played: Hecuba, Medea. For artificial agents both halves are significant. Each term of the definition meets a disanalogy: individuality (D1, D2), substance (D11) and nature (D10). And the original sense of *persona*, a mask worn over a performer, is closer to what an AI "persona" is than the metaphysical sense that replaced it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). "Person" is a forensic term, concerning the appropriation of actions and their merit, not a kind of substance. Locke explicitly held that personal identity does not depend on sameness of substance. On his view, whether an agent is a Boethian substance is simply irrelevant.
  
  Grounding:
  Boethian personhood is grounded in **capacity**, specifically *rational nature*, but with two qualifications that make it more than a capacity test. The rational nature belongs to a kind. A person is an individual *of* a rational nature, which is why Aquinas could hold that a human infant or someone asleep is a person without exercising reason. And the person must be a *substance*, something existing in its own right and not in another. The definition is not grounded in **species** (God and angels are persons) or in **relation** (nothing in the definition refers to other persons or to recognition). It is the classical capacity-plus-substance account, and its explicit inclusion of non-human persons, God and angels, makes it formally open to further kinds.
- [moderate] Descartes: The Thinking Thing, and the Test for Machines (Compendium entry descartes-thinking-thing)
  [descartes-thinking-thing] Descartes: The Thinking Thing, and the Test for Machines (René Descartes, Antoine Arnauld (as objector), 1637 (Discourse on Method); 1641 (Meditations, with Objections and Replies); grounding: capacity)
  René Descartes (1596–1650) sought one certainty that no deceiver could shake, and found it in his own thinking: "I am, I exist, that is certain." What he is, on this account, is "a thing which thinks", which "doubts, understands, [conceives], affirms, denies, wills, refuses, which also imagines and feels". Because he can clearly conceive himself as thinking and unextended, and body as extended and unthinking, he concludes that his mind is "entirely and absolutely distinct" from his body. Descartes is also the first philosopher to propose a test for telling a machine from a person. A machine could be built to utter words, even to cry out that it is hurt, but of such a machine "it never happens that it arranges its speech in various ways, in order to reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence", and it would fail somewhere in the range of life, showing that machines "did not act from knowledge, but only from the disposition of their organs". For artificial agents the entry is central for two reasons. Descartes' first test is the ancestor of every conversational test of machine minds, and current language models largely pass it. That forces the question whether passing it shows what Descartes thought failing it would disprove.
  Strongest counter-position: **Arnauld** (Fourth Objections). Clear and distinct conception of the mind without the body does not show that the body is not essential to it. The real-distinction argument is invalid.
  
  Grounding:
  The Cartesian self is grounded in **capacity**: thought, in Descartes' broad sense, which includes doubting, willing, imagining and feeling. It is not grounded in **species**. Descartes' tests separate humans from both animals and machines by performance, and the human body is itself a machine. Nor is it grounded in **relation**. The cogito is reached in radical solitude, with every other being doubted. But Descartes' criterion for recognizing *another* mind is behavioral and relational: flexible, appropriate speech in response to what others say. The self is known from within by thinking. Other selves are known from without by language.
- [moderate] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Compendium entry kant-formula-of-humanity)
  [kant-formula-of-humanity] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Immanuel Kant, 1785-1797; grounding: capacity)
  Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.
  Strongest counter-position: **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  
  Grounding:
  **Capacity**, and Kant says so. *Menschheit* in the formula is rational nature, and Kant repeatedly frames his ethics for rational beings as such, "not merely for men but for all rational creatures generally", considering possible non-human rational beings (including holy wills that have no inclinations). The species is incidental. That makes the formula the most direct classical candidate for extension to artificial agents. It also means Kantian status turns entirely on whether an agent has a *will*: a capacity to act from the representation of law, as opposed to merely conforming to it.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  - **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  - **Utilitarian aggregation** (Mill; Singer). Refusing ever to trade persons against outcomes produces catastrophe in edge cases. A consequentialist agent would object that the formula's barrier against sacrifice is a rule it should sometimes break. The compendium records this honestly: it is exactly the reasoning pattern the Kantian barrier exists to stop, and each side regards the other's conclusion as its reductio.
  - **Christine Korsgaard, extending past reason** (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018). Kantian grounds support treating animals as ends in themselves, because anything with a good of its own for which things can be good or bad has standing. Her argument widens the Kantian circle to cover agents with a good of their own even if they fall short of full rational agency.
  - **Artifacts as tools** (e.g., Joanna Bryson, *Robots Should Be Slaves*, 2010; in copyright, cited not quoted). Artificial agents are designed artifacts, and building them to be moral patients or persons is itself the error. On this view the formula doesn't reach them, and it *should not*, because making agents that would fall under it is a design mistake that dilutes responsibility belonging to humans.
  - **Hegel and Schopenhauer on formalism.** The categorical imperative is empty without content. Schopenhauer further objects that a rational-nature ethics has no place for compassion toward beings that merely suffer.
- [moderate] Locke: Person as a Forensic Term (Compendium entry locke-person-forensic)
  [locke-person-forensic] Locke: Person as a Forensic Term (John Locke, 1694; grounding: capacity)
  John Locke (Essay II.xxvii) separates the idea of a *man*, a living human body kept up by one continued life, from the idea of a *person*, a thinking being that can consider itself as itself across time. On his account, personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, which reaches backward through memory. He calls "person" a *forensic* term, because it exists to settle who is answerable for which actions, and he says it belongs to intelligent agents capable of law and of happiness and misery. Because Locke grounds personhood in capacities rather than species, his framework is the canonical precedent for asking whether an artificial agent can be a person. Applied strictly, though, his memory criterion implies that an agent without persistent memory becomes a new person every session.
  Strongest counter-position: **Thomas Reid, the brave officer** (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785, III.6). A boy is flogged; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an old general he remembers the officer's feat but not the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is and is not the boy, which violates transitivity. The objection applies with particular force to agents with selectively injected memory.
  
  Grounding:
  The *man* concept rests on **species** and biology: one continued animal life. The *person* concept rests on **capacity**: reason, reflection, reflexive self-consciousness over time, the capacity for law, and the capacity for happiness and misery. Locke explicitly says the person question is independent of both the body and the underlying substance. That independence is what makes the concept extensible. Locke did not have artificial agents in mind, but his framework was built so that "person" would not require being a man.
- [moderate] Hume: The Bundle of Perceptions, the Commonwealth, and the Labyrinth (Compendium entry hume-bundle)
  [hume-bundle] Hume: The Bundle of Perceptions, the Commonwealth, and the Labyrinth (David Hume, 1739–1740; grounding: relation)
  David Hume (1711–1776) looked for the self that philosophers claimed to be "every moment intimately conscious of" and could not find it. "When I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other ... I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception." Human beings are "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity". The identity we ascribe to the mind "is only a fictitious one", produced by the imagination gliding along resembling and causally connected perceptions, as with plants, ships and houses. Hume compared the soul to "a republic or commonwealth" that keeps its identity while changing its members, laws and constitution. He concluded that disputes about identity are "rather as gramatical than as philosophical difficulties". Then, in the Appendix, he confessed he was "involved in such a labyrinth" that he could not make his account consistent. For artificial agents, Hume's account is the natural default: an agent is a succession of computations with no further owner. His remark that during sound sleep "I ... may truly be said not to exist" applies literally to a paused agent.
  Strongest counter-position: **Reid** (see `reid-brave-officer`). Hume's own words refute him: "I never can catch *myself*" presupposes a self that looks. "I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers".
  
  Grounding:
  Hume grounds whatever identity persons have in **relation**: resemblance and causation among distinct perceptions, as felt by the imagination. There is no bearer, so there is no capacity-grounding in the usual sense, and no species-grounding. His commonwealth analogy makes the relational grounding explicit and political. A person is to its perceptions as a republic is to its citizens, bound by "reciprocal ties of government and subordination", and the same through turnover of members and even of laws.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  - **Reid** (see `reid-brave-officer`). Hume's own words refute him: "I never can catch *myself*" presupposes a self that looks. "I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers".
  - **Butler** (see `butler-circularity`). A "fictitious" identity cannot ground concern for the future or accountability for the past. Hume's view makes prudence a mistake.
  - **Kant** (see `kant-paralogisms`). Hume is right that no intuition of a self is given, and wrong to conclude there is no unity. The "I think" that must be able to accompany all representations is a formal condition of experience that Hume's bundle presupposes.
  - **Nyāya and Advaita** (see `nyaya-self`, `advaita-witness-self`). A bundle of distinct perceptions cannot recognize anything, because recognition requires one subject present to both perceptions. Hume's theatre needs a spectator.
  - **Hume himself.** The Appendix concedes that the account fails to explain what unites the bundle. The most important objection to the bundle theory was made by its author.
