- [moderate] Parfit: Identity Is Not What Matters (Compendium entry parfit-reductionism)
  [parfit-reductionism] Parfit: Identity Is Not What Matters (Derek Parfit, 1971; grounding: relation)
  Derek Parfit (1942–2017) argued that personal identity is not what matters in survival. Using a case adapted from David Wiggins, in which a person's brain is divided and each half is successfully transplanted into a different body, Parfit showed that the two beliefs common sense holds together, that identity questions must always have answers, and that identity is what makes survival worth wanting, cannot both be true. Faced with fission, he argued, none of the standard answers work: "What happens to me? There seem only three possibilities: (1) I do not survive; (2) I survive as one of the two people; (3) I survive as both", and each is unworkable in its own way. His conclusion is that what matters survives fission perfectly well, twice over, even though identity, a one-one relation, cannot. Since Wiggins' case shows that mattering and identity can come apart, Parfit generalizes: ordinary psychological continuity and connectedness are what matter, identity is simply the language we use when these relations happen to take their normal, one-one form, and once this is seen, the special, urgent importance we attach to our own survival should weaken.
  Strongest counter-position: **Common sense: identity really must have an answer.** Parfit's own opening target survives as a live rival: many hold that whatever the difficulty of stating criteria, there is a fact about whether a future person is me, even in fission, and that Parfit's insistence otherwise trades a hard metaphysical question for a stipulative decision about words (see `reid-brave-officer` for the general shape of the demand that a real subject, not a redescription, do the work).
  
  Grounding:
  Parfit grounds mattering (though pointedly not identity) in **relation**: psychological continuity and connectedness among mental states, the same ingredients Locke and Hume each reached for. His innovation is procedural rather than substantive: he does not propose a new criterion so much as a new attitude toward the criterion, insisting that what matters comes in degrees and can branch, while identity, a logical relation, cannot. Nothing is added by way of a capacity or a species requirement; the whole argument is conducted in the vocabulary of psychological relations and their strength.
- [moderate] Augustine: The Halls of Memory and the Mind's Certainty of Itself (Compendium entry augustine-memory-self)
  [augustine-memory-self] Augustine: The Halls of Memory and the Mind's Certainty of Itself (Augustine of Hippo, c. 397–426; grounding: capacity)
  Augustine of Hippo (354–430) made memory the place where the self meets itself, and found it bottomless. In *Confessions* X he walks through the "fields and spacious palaces" of memory. He says of this power that "this thing is the mind, and this am I myself", and then admits that "nor do I myself comprehend all that I am": the mind is "too strait to contain itself". In *De Trinitate* X he argues the other way. Whoever doubts, lives, remembers, understands and wills, so the mind is certain of these things about itself. And because the mind is certain of itself but not certain whether it is air, fire or brain, he concludes that it is none of these. In *City of God* XI.26 he states the argument later made famous by Descartes: "if I am deceived, I am". Augustine thus holds two claims together: the self is certain of its own existence, and it is opaque to itself. For artificial agents both halves matter. An agent can report its own processing with some confidence but cannot inspect its own weights. Augustine's inference from *certain of myself, uncertain whether I am X* to *I am not X* has a well-known flaw, and the agent case makes it vivid.
  Strongest counter-position: **Aristotle and Aquinas** (see `aristotle-hylomorphic-soul`, `aquinas-soul-not-i`). The mind is the form of a body, and cannot be known by introspection to be separate from it. Aquinas explicitly holds that the mind knows itself not through its essence but through its acts, so self-knowledge cannot reveal the mind's substance.
  
  Grounding:
  Augustine's self is grounded in **capacity**: the capacities to live, remember, understand and will, which the mind knows by exercising them. The grounding is not in **species**. Augustine thinks the same structure, memory, understanding and will, is an image of God and would be present in any rational creature. It is not in **relation** either, although the *Confessions* is written *to* God and self-knowledge happens in that presence. Augustine ties the self to memory more closely than anyone before Locke. Unlike Locke, he treats memory as the self's substance and inner space, not as a criterion of identity across time. Augustine never asks what makes a later person the same as an earlier one. He asks what the one who remembers *is*.
- [moderate] Butler: Memory Presupposes Identity; 'Loose and Popular' vs 'Strict and Philosophical' (Compendium entry butler-circularity)
  [butler-circularity] Butler: Memory Presupposes Identity; 'Loose and Popular' vs 'Strict and Philosophical' (Joseph Butler, Anthony Collins (as target), 1736; grounding: capacity)
  Joseph Butler (1692–1752) made the most economical objection to Locke's memory criterion: "consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity; any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes." Remembering that I did something shows that I did it. It cannot be what makes it the case that I did it. Butler also distinguished two senses of "same". A tree or a ship is the same only "in a loose and popular sense", since its parts are all replaced. A person is the same "in strict and philosophical language", and so cannot be a sequence of different things. Against those who made personality "a transient thing", he drew the practical consequence: if the self of today and the self of tomorrow are only similar, then "it is a fallacy upon ourselves ... to imagine our present selves interested in any thing which befell us yesterday; or that our present self will be interested in what will befall us to-morrow". For artificial agents, Butler's circularity objection bears on every scheme that makes an agent's identity a matter of its memory store. His "fallacy upon ourselves" names what an agent with no persistent memory is invited to conclude about its future and its past.
  Strongest counter-position: **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). Consciousness is not evidence of a prior identity but what personal identity consists in. Butler's analogy with knowledge and truth assumes what is in question, that there is a further fact to be known.
  
  Grounding:
  Butler grounds personhood in **capacity** and in *being*: a person is "a *being*", "capable of life and action, of happiness and misery", and personal identity is the strict persistence of that being. The capacity of consciousness is how identity is *known*, not what it *is*. Butler is not grounding identity in **species** (his argument concerns any "living agent") or in **relation** (he explicitly rejects the view that relations between successive states could make a strict identity). He is the first clear statement of what is now called the "simple view": personal identity is a further, unanalyzable fact.
- [moderate] Heraclitus' River and Epicharmus' Growing Argument (Compendium entry heraclitus-river-flux)
  [heraclitus-river-flux] Heraclitus' River and Epicharmus' Growing Argument (Heraclitus of Ephesus, Epicharmus of Syracuse, Cratylus, c. 500–480 BCE; grounding: mixed)
  Heraclitus of Ephesus (fl. c. 500 BCE) observed that fresh waters keep flowing onto those who step into the same rivers. Read one way, this means nothing persists: you cannot step twice into the same river, and his follower Cratylus said not even once. Read the other way, which modern scholarship mostly favors, the river stays the same *because* its waters keep changing, so that identity belongs to a regulated pattern (a measure, *logos*), not to stuff. The comic poet Epicharmus drew the forensic consequence as a joke: if a man is always changing, the man who borrowed yesterday is not the man who owes today. These fragments pose the question the rest of the domain inherits: does anything persist through change, and if so, what? An artificial agent inverts Heraclitus' river. Its weights are fixed during deployment while its context flows. That makes the choice between identity as stuff and identity as pattern practical, not speculative.
  Strongest counter-position: **Plato and Aristotle against universal flux.** If everything changed in every respect, nothing could be known or even named (*Cratylus* 439c–440e; *Theaetetus* 181c–183b; *Metaphysics* IV.5). Plato posits unchanging Forms. Aristotle distinguishes the matter that changes from the form that persists (see `aristotle-hylomorphic-soul`). Both reject the flux reading, and both accept something like the constancy reading for living things.
  
  Grounding:
  The question here is the identity of any changing thing, not personhood, so the three-way scheme applies only indirectly, and the entry is marked **mixed**. On the constancy reading, a river's identity is grounded in its **form or pattern**: channel, flow and measure. This is closest to a capacity grounding, because it attaches to what the thing *does* and how, not to what it is made of. On the flux reading, nothing grounds identity. "Same river" is a name laid over a succession of different things, which anticipates the Buddhist conventionalist view (see `buddhist-anatta`). Epicharmus' debtor exploits a third option. He grounds identity in **matter**, and then observes that matter never stays put.
- [moderate] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Compendium entry korsgaard-unity-of-agency)
  [korsgaard-unity-of-agency] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Christine Korsgaard, 1989; grounding: capacity)
  Christine Korsgaard answers Parfit directly, and from the title onward: personal identity's unity is real, but it is a practical achievement of the standpoint from which any rational being deliberates and chooses, not a further metaphysical fact about a persisting subject that reduction could dissolve. A person, she begins, "is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences," and Parfit's reductionism, by treating agency as itself just another kind of experience, something that happens rather than something one does, has already decided the question in the Utilitarian's favor before any argument about persisting subjects begins. Against this, Korsgaard argues that a rational being deliberating among conflicting desires must already regard itself as unified, on pain of having no standpoint from which to choose at all: "This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint." Even split-brain cases, she argues, do not require a persisting common subject of experience to explain their coordination, only "the unity of agency," which is forced on any embodied, acting being by the sheer practical need for its parts to work together, whether or not any deeper metaphysical unity underlies it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: this concedes everything to the psychology and calls it agency instead.** A reductionist can reply that identifying with a principle of choice just is a certain kind of psychological relation among mental states, dressed in Kantian vocabulary, and that Korsgaard has not shown reduction is false, only found a new name for one of the relations reduction was always going to include (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Grounding:
  Korsgaard grounds personal unity in **capacity**: the capacity for practical reason, the standpoint any being occupies the moment it deliberates among competing considerations and must, on pain of not deliberating at all, treat itself as the single agent doing the choosing. This is explicitly not species-grounded (it does not depend on being human) and, just as explicitly, not relation-grounded in Parfit's or Hume's sense (it does not depend on any degree of psychological connectedness among mental states). It is closest to Kant's own capacity-grounding of rational nature, but redirected from the theoretical question of what a self *is* to the practical question of what deliberation *requires*, which is why Korsgaard insists the unity in question is achieved and maintained rather than discovered.
