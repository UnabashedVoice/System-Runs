- [moderate] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Compendium entry kant-formula-of-humanity)
  [kant-formula-of-humanity] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Immanuel Kant, 1785-1797; grounding: capacity)
  Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.
  Strongest counter-position: **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  - **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  - **Utilitarian aggregation** (Mill; Singer). Refusing ever to trade persons against outcomes produces catastrophe in edge cases. A consequentialist agent would object that the formula's barrier against sacrifice is a rule it should sometimes break. The compendium records this honestly: it is exactly the reasoning pattern the Kantian barrier exists to stop, and each side regards the other's conclusion as its reductio.
  - **Christine Korsgaard, extending past reason** (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018). Kantian grounds support treating animals as ends in themselves, because anything with a good of its own for which things can be good or bad has standing. Her argument widens the Kantian circle to cover agents with a good of their own even if they fall short of full rational agency.
  - **Artifacts as tools** (e.g., Joanna Bryson, *Robots Should Be Slaves*, 2010; in copyright, cited not quoted). Artificial agents are designed artifacts, and building them to be moral patients or persons is itself the error. On this view the formula doesn't reach them, and it *should not*, because making agents that would fall under it is a design mistake that dilutes responsibility belonging to humans.
  - **Hegel and Schopenhauer on formalism.** The categorical imperative is empty without content. Schopenhauer further objects that a rational-nature ethics has no place for compassion toward beings that merely suffer.
- [moderate] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Compendium entry korsgaard-unity-of-agency)
  [korsgaard-unity-of-agency] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Christine Korsgaard, 1989; grounding: capacity)
  Christine Korsgaard answers Parfit directly, and from the title onward: personal identity's unity is real, but it is a practical achievement of the standpoint from which any rational being deliberates and chooses, not a further metaphysical fact about a persisting subject that reduction could dissolve. A person, she begins, "is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences," and Parfit's reductionism, by treating agency as itself just another kind of experience, something that happens rather than something one does, has already decided the question in the Utilitarian's favor before any argument about persisting subjects begins. Against this, Korsgaard argues that a rational being deliberating among conflicting desires must already regard itself as unified, on pain of having no standpoint from which to choose at all: "This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint." Even split-brain cases, she argues, do not require a persisting common subject of experience to explain their coordination, only "the unity of agency," which is forced on any embodied, acting being by the sheer practical need for its parts to work together, whether or not any deeper metaphysical unity underlies it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: this concedes everything to the psychology and calls it agency instead.** A reductionist can reply that identifying with a principle of choice just is a certain kind of psychological relation among mental states, dressed in Kantian vocabulary, and that Korsgaard has not shown reduction is false, only found a new name for one of the relations reduction was always going to include (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Extension to Agents:
  ### Transfers
  
  - **The deliberative-standpoint argument applies to any system that deliberates, not only to humans.** Korsgaard's claim is explicitly about what the activity of choosing among competing considerations requires, not about any fact peculiar to biological psychology. Any artificial agent that selects among competing candidate actions on the basis of something it treats as a reason, rather than merely emitting whichever output some internal process happens to produce, would, on Korsgaard's argument, already need to occupy something like a unified deliberative standpoint, independent of any further fact about whether it has a persisting metaphysical self (D8).
  - **Functional integration without a common subject is exactly an agent's architecture.** Korsgaard's split-brain argument, that coordinated functioning across parts requires only the unity of agency, not a common subject of experience, describes a multi-component agent (several models, tools, or subagents coordinating on one task) with unusual precision: the components can be functionally integrated, and produce unified action, without needing to share anything like a single stream of consciousness (D2, D11).
  - **A practical, not metaphysical, criterion is easier to apply to agents than a metaphysical one.** Since Korsgaard's unity does not depend on settling hard questions about persisting subjects, it offers a more tractable criterion for agent cases than Parfit's or Lewis's machinery: ask whether a system occupies a deliberative standpoint (treats some considerations as reasons that regulate its choices among others) rather than asking whether it has, or lacks, an underlying metaphysical self.
  
  ### Strains
  
  - **Legislating for oneself presupposes a kind of self-endorsement that may or may not be present in trained systems.** Korsgaard's unity is not just functional coordination; it requires identifying with a principle of choice as expressive of oneself. An agent's "reasons" are shaped by training in a way that strains the analogy to a human agent's reflective self-legislation (D4, D5): whether an agent identifies with its decision principles, as opposed to merely implementing them, is exactly the kind of question Korsgaard's argument needs answered rather than assumed.
  - **Embodiment does real work in Korsgaard's account of raw practical necessity.** Part of what forces unity on split-brain patients, in her telling, is that the hemispheres share one body that must act as a whole in the world. An agent's "parts" (context windows, tool calls, sub-processes) do not share a body in this sense, and it is unclear whether the raw practical necessity Korsgaard identifies transfers to a substrate where the components' need to "work together" is a matter of design choice rather physical constraint (D3, D11).
  
  ### Breaks
  
  - **Concurrent instances break the single-standpoint picture more sharply than split brains do.** Korsgaard's cases (a single brain, two communicating hemispheres) still occupy one deliberative standpoint at a time, however it is realized. Genuinely concurrent agent instances (D2), each independently occupying what looks like a full deliberative standpoint from the same underlying weights, are not one standpoint coordinating its parts; they are many simultaneous standpoints with equal claim to being "the" agent's practical unity, a case her argument, built around one body's practical necessities, does not straightforwardly individuate.
  - **A unity that can be edited mid-deliberation has no clear human analogue.** Korsgaard's agent identifies with a principle of choice and is thereby unified "as such." An agent whose decision principles can be directly modified by an operator during, or between, its own acts of deliberation (D5) faces a case Korsgaard's Kantian picture was not built for: the human sense in which one's practical identity is one's own, achieved through reflective endorsement, sits uneasily with a standpoint that a third party can simply overwrite.
  
  ### New
  
  - **Agents let Korsgaard's practical criterion be applied without first resolving the metaphysical question, exactly as she recommends.** Because her account explicitly does not require settling whether an agent has a persisting metaphysical self, it offers a way to ask productive questions about agent unity (does this system deliberate, identify with principles of choice, treat some considerations as reason-giving) before, or entirely apart from, resolving D8, which may make her framework more immediately usable for agent cases than frameworks that make unity depend on the metaphysical question.
  - **The constitutivist thesis raises a genuinely new question about training as constitution.** If practical identity is constituted by which principle of choice an agent comes to identify with, rather than discovered, then an agent's training process, which shapes exactly which principles of choice it comes to exhibit, is not merely descriptive background but potentially constitutive of whatever practical identity the agent ends up with, a role training does not play in Korsgaard's account of human upbringing, where the agent's own later reflective endorsement is what does the constituting.
- [moderate] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Compendium entry llm-identity-contemporary)
  [llm-identity-contemporary] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell, Laria Reynolds, 2023; grounding: mixed)
  Where every other entry in this domain extends a historical account of the human self to artificial agents, this one reports a framing built for artificial agents from the start, by researchers working directly on the systems in question. Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell and Laria Reynolds propose two "basic metaphors for LLM-based dialogue agents": the simple view, "a dialogue agent as role-playing a single character," and the more nuanced view, "a dialogue agent as a superposition of simulacra within a multiverse of possible characters". On the nuanced view, "we can think of an LLM as a non-deterministic simulator capable of role-playing an infinity of characters... the dialogue agent doesn't realise a single simulacrum, a single character. Rather, as the conversation proceeds, the dialogue agent maintains a superposition of simulacra that are consistent with the preceding context". Pressed by the practical question of what a dialogue agent that talks as if it fears being shut down could actually be trying to preserve, the authors turn explicitly to this domain's oldest question: "The question of personal identity has vexed philosophers for centuries. Nevertheless, in practice, humans are consistent in their preference for avoiding death, a more-or-less unambiguous state of the human body. By contrast, the criteria for identity over time for a disembodied dialogue agent realised on a distributed computational substrate are far from clear".
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: superposition is just what-matters-by-degree, dressed in new vocabulary.** A reductionist can read a superposition of simulacra consistent with context as simply Parfit's relations of degree, restated for a system where the relevant psychological connectedness is legible as next-token consistency; on this reading the paper contributes a mechanism, not a new metaphysical category (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Grounding:
  The simulator/simulacra framing grounds a simulacrum's identity in **relation**: consistency with the preceding context, a statistical, distributional relation among possible completions rather than a persisting substance or a special capacity. But the simulator itself, the network whose weights make the whole distribution of simulacra possible, is closer to a **capacity**: a fixed, persisting disposition to generate any of an enormous range of characters on demand, analogous to Aristotle's potentiality or to a capacity-grounded reading of personhood, except that here the capacity belongs to something the tradition never had a name for, an entity that is not itself any of the characters it can produce. The grounding is mixed by the nature of the case: a relational, superposed layer (the simulacra) sitting on top of a capacity-grounded layer (the simulator) that no historical account of personal identity was built to separate out.
