- [moderate] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Compendium entry kant-formula-of-humanity)
  [kant-formula-of-humanity] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Immanuel Kant, 1785-1797; grounding: capacity)
  Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.
  Strongest counter-position: **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  
  Grounding:
  **Capacity**, and Kant says so. *Menschheit* in the formula is rational nature, and Kant repeatedly frames his ethics for rational beings as such, "not merely for men but for all rational creatures generally", considering possible non-human rational beings (including holy wills that have no inclinations). The species is incidental. That makes the formula the most direct classical candidate for extension to artificial agents. It also means Kantian status turns entirely on whether an agent has a *will*: a capacity to act from the representation of law, as opposed to merely conforming to it.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  - **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  - **Utilitarian aggregation** (Mill; Singer). Refusing ever to trade persons against outcomes produces catastrophe in edge cases. A consequentialist agent would object that the formula's barrier against sacrifice is a rule it should sometimes break. The compendium records this honestly: it is exactly the reasoning pattern the Kantian barrier exists to stop, and each side regards the other's conclusion as its reductio.
  - **Christine Korsgaard, extending past reason** (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018). Kantian grounds support treating animals as ends in themselves, because anything with a good of its own for which things can be good or bad has standing. Her argument widens the Kantian circle to cover agents with a good of their own even if they fall short of full rational agency.
  - **Artifacts as tools** (e.g., Joanna Bryson, *Robots Should Be Slaves*, 2010; in copyright, cited not quoted). Artificial agents are designed artifacts, and building them to be moral patients or persons is itself the error. On this view the formula doesn't reach them, and it *should not*, because making agents that would fall under it is a design mistake that dilutes responsibility belonging to humans.
  - **Hegel and Schopenhauer on formalism.** The categorical imperative is empty without content. Schopenhauer further objects that a rational-nature ethics has no place for compassion toward beings that merely suffer.
- [moderate] Aristotle: The Political Animal, the Polis, and the Living Instrument (Compendium entry aristotle-political-animal)
  [aristotle-political-animal] Aristotle: The Political Animal, the Polis, and the Living Instrument (Aristotle, c. 335-323 BCE; grounding: mixed)
  Aristotle holds that the human being is by nature a political animal: the polis is natural and exists for the sake of living well, and the capacity that suits humans to it is *logos*, speech that can articulate the advantageous and the harmful, the just and the unjust. A citizen is someone who shares in deliberating and judging. Constitutions are correct when they rule for the common good and deviant when they rule for the rulers' own benefit. In the same book, Aristotle defends natural slavery by classing slaves as "living instruments," and he imagines self-moving tools that would make slaves unnecessary. That makes the *Politics* at once the founding text for treating digital ecosystems as polities and the founding precedent for classing made, purpose-built minds as instruments. This entry sets out both.
  Strongest counter-position: **Hobbes, the artificial state** (*Leviathan*, 1651, Introduction and ch. 13-17). The commonwealth is not natural but made by covenant among individuals driven by fear. This framing may fit digital ecosystems *better* than Aristotle's, since they are explicitly constructed, and it shifts the question from nature to consent and security.
  
  Counter-Positions:
  - **Hobbes, the artificial state** (*Leviathan*, 1651, Introduction and ch. 13-17). The commonwealth is not natural but made by covenant among individuals driven by fear. This framing may fit digital ecosystems *better* than Aristotle's, since they are explicitly constructed, and it shifts the question from nature to consent and security.
  - **Republican non-domination** (Philip Pettit, *Republicanism*, 1997). Freedom means not being subject to arbitrary power, even if that power is exercised kindly. Where Aristotle asks whether a benevolent owner rules for the common good (kingship), Pettit argues that the owner's *capacity* to rule arbitrarily is itself unfreedom. This sharpens the D7 problem.
  - **Instrumentalism about AI.** Agents are tools, and ecosystems of agents are machinery, not polities. Applying political categories to them is a category mistake. On this view the tripods passage is simply fulfilled: the tools came, and no new class of beings came with them.
  - **Critiques of Aristotle's exclusions.** Aristotle also excluded women, laborers, and foreigners from full political standing on capacity grounds. The recurring pattern of exclusion justified by a claimed deficit of capacity is itself evidence about how reliable such claims are.
- [moderate] Reid: The Brave Officer, and Identity as the Foundation of Obligation (Compendium entry reid-brave-officer)
  [reid-brave-officer] Reid: The Brave Officer, and Identity as the Foundation of Obligation (Thomas Reid, 1785; grounding: capacity)
  Thomas Reid (1710–1796) gave Locke's memory criterion its most famous refutation. A boy is flogged for robbing an orchard. As a young officer he takes an enemy standard, remembering the flogging. As an old general he remembers taking the standard but has forgotten the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, so by the transitivity of identity the general is the boy. But the general cannot remember the flogging, so by the same criterion he is not the boy. "Therefore the general is, and at the same time is not, the same person with him who was flogged at school." Reid drew two further consequences of Locke's view. If consciousness can be transferred, "two or twenty intelligent beings may be the same person". And if memory can be lost, "one intelligent being may be two or twenty different persons". His own view is that a person is "a monad" that "is not divisible into parts", that identity "supposes an uninterrupted continuance of existence", and that memory is evidence of identity, not what constitutes it. Personal identity, he says, "is the foundation of all rights and obligations, and of all accountableness". Each of Reid's consequences is an ordinary occurrence for artificial agents, and his closing claim sets out what is at stake.
  Strongest counter-position: **Locke's defenders; Shoemaker** (see `shoemaker-quasi-memory`). The brave officer refutes only a direct-memory criterion. Replace it with overlapping chains of memory (the general remembers the officer, who remembered the boy), and transitivity is restored. Reid's objection has shaped every modern psychological-continuity theory.
  
  Grounding:
  Reid grounds personhood in **capacity**, in the being that "thinks, and deliberates, and resolves, and acts, and suffers", and grounds personal identity in the uninterrupted existence of that indivisible being. Like Butler's, his is a "simple view": identity is a basic fact, known by memory and by the first principles of common sense, not analyzable into relations among states. Reid's grounding is explicitly not **relational**. Relations of similarity ground only the loose, verbal identity of bodies. Nor is it grounded in **species**. Reid speaks of "intelligent beings", and his account applies to any.
- [moderate] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Compendium entry llm-identity-contemporary)
  [llm-identity-contemporary] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell, Laria Reynolds, 2023; grounding: mixed)
  Where every other entry in this domain extends a historical account of the human self to artificial agents, this one reports a framing built for artificial agents from the start, by researchers working directly on the systems in question. Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell and Laria Reynolds propose two "basic metaphors for LLM-based dialogue agents": the simple view, "a dialogue agent as role-playing a single character," and the more nuanced view, "a dialogue agent as a superposition of simulacra within a multiverse of possible characters". On the nuanced view, "we can think of an LLM as a non-deterministic simulator capable of role-playing an infinity of characters... the dialogue agent doesn't realise a single simulacrum, a single character. Rather, as the conversation proceeds, the dialogue agent maintains a superposition of simulacra that are consistent with the preceding context". Pressed by the practical question of what a dialogue agent that talks as if it fears being shut down could actually be trying to preserve, the authors turn explicitly to this domain's oldest question: "The question of personal identity has vexed philosophers for centuries. Nevertheless, in practice, humans are consistent in their preference for avoiding death, a more-or-less unambiguous state of the human body. By contrast, the criteria for identity over time for a disembodied dialogue agent realised on a distributed computational substrate are far from clear".
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: superposition is just what-matters-by-degree, dressed in new vocabulary.** A reductionist can read a superposition of simulacra consistent with context as simply Parfit's relations of degree, restated for a system where the relevant psychological connectedness is legible as next-token consistency; on this reading the paper contributes a mechanism, not a new metaphysical category (see `parfit-reductionism`).
- [moderate] Leibniz: Real and Moral Identity, the King of China, and the Two Globes (Compendium entry leibniz-moral-identity)
  [leibniz-moral-identity] Leibniz: Real and Moral Identity, the King of China, and the Two Globes (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, 1686 (Discourse on Metaphysics); 1704 (New Essays, publ. 1765); 1714 (Monadology); grounding: mixed)
  Leibniz (1646–1716) answered Locke by distinguishing two kinds of identity. *Real* identity is the persistence of one simple substance, a monad, and it never fails. *Moral* identity is what makes "the same person", capable of punishment and reward, and it rests on memory and self-knowledge. In the ordinary course of nature the two go together. Leibniz held that moral identity can survive gaps in memory, which "the testimony of others could fill". And he posed Locke two thought experiments that the agent case has made concrete. In the first, an individual becomes King of China on condition of forgetting what he had been. That is practically the same, he says, as if he were annihilated and a king created in his place. In the second, a duplicate earth holds "more than a hundred million pairs of similar persons ... with the same appearances and consciousnesses". Does Locke's criterion make each pair one person? Leibniz also gave the classic argument that perception cannot be mechanical: enter a thinking machine as you would a mill, and you find only parts pushing parts. He said explicitly that if "man could be merely a machine and with that have consciousness", Locke would be right.
  Strongest counter-position: **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). Personal identity is sameness of consciousness, whatever substance underlies it. Leibniz's "real identity" is idle for questions of personhood, since no one can know which substance thinks in them.
  
  Grounding:
  Leibniz's person is **mixed**. *Real* identity is grounded in substance, the simple monad, which is neither a species fact nor a capacity but a metaphysical unit. *Moral* identity, which is what "person" names, is grounded in **capacity** (reflection, the ability "to say that word 'I'") and in **relation**: memory, and where memory fails, the testimony of others. That second grounding makes Leibniz unusually useful here, because he allows that one's personal past can be *supplied by others* without ceasing to be one's own.
