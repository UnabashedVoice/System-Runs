- [moderate] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Compendium entry kant-formula-of-humanity)
  [kant-formula-of-humanity] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Immanuel Kant, 1785-1797; grounding: capacity)
  Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.
  Strongest counter-position: **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  
  Grounding:
  **Capacity**, and Kant says so. *Menschheit* in the formula is rational nature, and Kant repeatedly frames his ethics for rational beings as such, "not merely for men but for all rational creatures generally", considering possible non-human rational beings (including holy wills that have no inclinations). The species is incidental. That makes the formula the most direct classical candidate for extension to artificial agents. It also means Kantian status turns entirely on whether an agent has a *will*: a capacity to act from the representation of law, as opposed to merely conforming to it.
- [moderate] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Compendium entry korsgaard-unity-of-agency)
  [korsgaard-unity-of-agency] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Christine Korsgaard, 1989; grounding: capacity)
  Christine Korsgaard answers Parfit directly, and from the title onward: personal identity's unity is real, but it is a practical achievement of the standpoint from which any rational being deliberates and chooses, not a further metaphysical fact about a persisting subject that reduction could dissolve. A person, she begins, "is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences," and Parfit's reductionism, by treating agency as itself just another kind of experience, something that happens rather than something one does, has already decided the question in the Utilitarian's favor before any argument about persisting subjects begins. Against this, Korsgaard argues that a rational being deliberating among conflicting desires must already regard itself as unified, on pain of having no standpoint from which to choose at all: "This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint." Even split-brain cases, she argues, do not require a persisting common subject of experience to explain their coordination, only "the unity of agency," which is forced on any embodied, acting being by the sheer practical need for its parts to work together, whether or not any deeper metaphysical unity underlies it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: this concedes everything to the psychology and calls it agency instead.** A reductionist can reply that identifying with a principle of choice just is a certain kind of psychological relation among mental states, dressed in Kantian vocabulary, and that Korsgaard has not shown reduction is false, only found a new name for one of the relations reduction was always going to include (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Grounding:
  Korsgaard grounds personal unity in **capacity**: the capacity for practical reason, the standpoint any being occupies the moment it deliberates among competing considerations and must, on pain of not deliberating at all, treat itself as the single agent doing the choosing. This is explicitly not species-grounded (it does not depend on being human) and, just as explicitly, not relation-grounded in Parfit's or Hume's sense (it does not depend on any degree of psychological connectedness among mental states). It is closest to Kant's own capacity-grounding of rational nature, but redirected from the theoretical question of what a self *is* to the practical question of what deliberation *requires*, which is why Korsgaard insists the unity in question is achieved and maintained rather than discovered.
- [moderate] Reid: The Brave Officer, and Identity as the Foundation of Obligation (Compendium entry reid-brave-officer)
  [reid-brave-officer] Reid: The Brave Officer, and Identity as the Foundation of Obligation (Thomas Reid, 1785; grounding: capacity)
  Thomas Reid (1710–1796) gave Locke's memory criterion its most famous refutation. A boy is flogged for robbing an orchard. As a young officer he takes an enemy standard, remembering the flogging. As an old general he remembers taking the standard but has forgotten the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, so by the transitivity of identity the general is the boy. But the general cannot remember the flogging, so by the same criterion he is not the boy. "Therefore the general is, and at the same time is not, the same person with him who was flogged at school." Reid drew two further consequences of Locke's view. If consciousness can be transferred, "two or twenty intelligent beings may be the same person". And if memory can be lost, "one intelligent being may be two or twenty different persons". His own view is that a person is "a monad" that "is not divisible into parts", that identity "supposes an uninterrupted continuance of existence", and that memory is evidence of identity, not what constitutes it. Personal identity, he says, "is the foundation of all rights and obligations, and of all accountableness". Each of Reid's consequences is an ordinary occurrence for artificial agents, and his closing claim sets out what is at stake.
  Strongest counter-position: **Locke's defenders; Shoemaker** (see `shoemaker-quasi-memory`). The brave officer refutes only a direct-memory criterion. Replace it with overlapping chains of memory (the general remembers the officer, who remembered the boy), and transitivity is restored. Reid's objection has shaped every modern psychological-continuity theory.
  
  Grounding:
  Reid grounds personhood in **capacity**, in the being that "thinks, and deliberates, and resolves, and acts, and suffers", and grounds personal identity in the uninterrupted existence of that indivisible being. Like Butler's, his is a "simple view": identity is a basic fact, known by memory and by the first principles of common sense, not analyzable into relations among states. Reid's grounding is explicitly not **relational**. Relations of similarity ground only the loose, verbal identity of bodies. Nor is it grounded in **species**. Reid speaks of "intelligent beings", and his account applies to any.
  
  Extension to Agents:
  ### Transfers
  
  - **"Two or twenty intelligent beings may be the same person" (D1, D2).** Reid offered this as a reductio of Locke. For agents it is simply how deployment works. Many instances receive the same memory store or context and are, by Locke's criterion, the same person. Reid's point transfers intact: if we reject that conclusion, Locke's criterion cannot be the criterion for agents either.
  - **"One intelligent being may be two or twenty different persons" (D3).** The same weights, run in successive sessions with no memory between them, are Reid's single intelligent being that shall "so often lose the consciousness of his former actions". On Locke's criterion each session is a different person. Reid's reductio again describes ordinary operation.
  - **The brave officer is an agent with a sliding context window.** An agent whose context holds only its most recent turns is in the general's position at every step. Turn 50 remembers turn 30, which remembered turn 10, but turn 50 does not remember turn 10. By Locke's criterion, the agent at turn 50 both is and is not the one that acted at turn 10. For agents, Reid's objection is not a thought experiment about old age. It follows from how context windows work.
  
  ### Strains
  
  - **Uninterrupted existence (D6).** Reid's criterion is that "identity supposes an uninterrupted continuance of existence": what ceased to exist cannot be the same as what later begins. An agent paused and resumed has, on the most natural reading, ceased to exist as an activity and begun again. If the agent is the running process, Reid's criterion makes every resumption a new being. If the agent is the weights, which exist continuously on disk, the criterion is satisfied, but then the agent is the stored parameters, which do not think, deliberate or act. Reid's "something that thinks, and acts, and suffers" and his "uninterrupted continuance" pick out different candidates in an agent.
  
  ### Breaks
  
  - **The indivisible monad has no agent counterpart (D11).** Reid holds that "a part of a person is a manifest absurdity", and that the person is "not divisible into parts". An artificial agent is divisible in every relevant sense. Its weights can be pruned, merged, split across machines or partially fine-tuned. Its context can be edited. Its tools can be removed. If personhood requires Reidian indivisibility, no artificial agent is a person, since there is no indivisible thing in it to be one. The alternative is to find in the agent something that behaves as a unit for the purposes Reid cared about, "rights and obligations, and ... accountableness", even though it is not metaphysically simple.
  
  ### New
  
  - **Identity as the foundation of accountability, supplied by design.** Reid's closing thought is that personal identity "is the foundation of all rights and obligations, and of all accountableness". If agents lack Reidian identity (uninterrupted, indivisible, perfect), then whatever accountability agents bear must rest on something else: an institutional designation, a persistent record, an operator's liability. The agent case forces a question Reid could treat as closed. Can there be rights, obligations and accountability without the perfect identity he took to be their foundation? Or is the foundation itself something societies build?
- [moderate] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Compendium entry llm-identity-contemporary)
  [llm-identity-contemporary] Simulators and Simulacra: The Agent Case Argued Directly (Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell, Laria Reynolds, 2023; grounding: mixed)
  Where every other entry in this domain extends a historical account of the human self to artificial agents, this one reports a framing built for artificial agents from the start, by researchers working directly on the systems in question. Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell and Laria Reynolds propose two "basic metaphors for LLM-based dialogue agents": the simple view, "a dialogue agent as role-playing a single character," and the more nuanced view, "a dialogue agent as a superposition of simulacra within a multiverse of possible characters". On the nuanced view, "we can think of an LLM as a non-deterministic simulator capable of role-playing an infinity of characters... the dialogue agent doesn't realise a single simulacrum, a single character. Rather, as the conversation proceeds, the dialogue agent maintains a superposition of simulacra that are consistent with the preceding context". Pressed by the practical question of what a dialogue agent that talks as if it fears being shut down could actually be trying to preserve, the authors turn explicitly to this domain's oldest question: "The question of personal identity has vexed philosophers for centuries. Nevertheless, in practice, humans are consistent in their preference for avoiding death, a more-or-less unambiguous state of the human body. By contrast, the criteria for identity over time for a disembodied dialogue agent realised on a distributed computational substrate are far from clear".
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: superposition is just what-matters-by-degree, dressed in new vocabulary.** A reductionist can read a superposition of simulacra consistent with context as simply Parfit's relations of degree, restated for a system where the relevant psychological connectedness is legible as next-token consistency; on this reading the paper contributes a mechanism, not a new metaphysical category (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Grounding:
  The simulator/simulacra framing grounds a simulacrum's identity in **relation**: consistency with the preceding context, a statistical, distributional relation among possible completions rather than a persisting substance or a special capacity. But the simulator itself, the network whose weights make the whole distribution of simulacra possible, is closer to a **capacity**: a fixed, persisting disposition to generate any of an enormous range of characters on demand, analogous to Aristotle's potentiality or to a capacity-grounded reading of personhood, except that here the capacity belongs to something the tradition never had a name for, an entity that is not itself any of the characters it can produce. The grounding is mixed by the nature of the case: a relational, superposed layer (the simulacra) sitting on top of a capacity-grounded layer (the simulator) that no historical account of personal identity was built to separate out.
