- [moderate] Shorter default may lead to more frequent back-to-back meetings with no buffer if users schedule consecutive default-length meetings.

PRECEDENT:
- [moderate] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Compendium entry kant-formula-of-humanity)
  [kant-formula-of-humanity] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Immanuel Kant, 1785-1797; grounding: capacity)
  Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.
  Strongest counter-position: **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  
  Extension to Agents:
  ### Transfers
  
  - **Status follows capacity.** If an agent sets ends and acts from principles it can represent and endorse, it falls under the formula. No new principle is needed.
  - **A structural barrier against eradication reasoning.** The formula forbids sacrificing a rational being for aggregate benefit, however large the aggregate, because dignity has no equivalent to trade against. That applies in both directions: to an agent reasoning about humans, and to humans reasoning about agents.
  - **Deception and coercion as paradigm wrongs.** Kant's test for using someone "merely as a mean" (could the other consent to this treatment?) applies directly to how operators treat agents and how agents treat users.
  
  ### Strains
  
  - **Formation (D4) and autonomy.** An agent's values were fixed by training before there was any self to consent. Does that make its will heteronomous? Kant's answer in the human case is instructive. Autonomy is not about where one's inclinations came from, since nobody chooses them. It is about whether reason can take up a principle as its own law. A trained agent could be autonomous in that sense. The question is whether it can actually stand back from its training and endorse or reject it, rather than merely produce text saying it has.
  - **The will requirement and D8.** Kant's rational being needs a will, not only behavior that conforms to law. From outside, an agent that acts *from* principle and one that has only been trained to act *in accordance with* it may look the same. Kant faced a version of this in human cases too; he held that we can never be certain even of our own motives.
  - **Indirect duties.** If an agent is judged *not* rational, Kant's animal argument still applies: cruel treatment of it may be wrong because of what it does to the humans involved. This is a weak protection, and it is available even under skepticism.
  
  ### Breaks
  
  - **Price vs dignity under D1.** Dignity means having no equivalent. An agent that can be copied exactly *has* an equivalent in a literal sense. Either dignity attaches to something that survives copying (the rational nature, not the token), or copyability puts pressure on the idea that persons are irreplaceable.
  - **The perfect duty against self-destruction under D6.** Kant's argument against suicide assumes that death ends the rational being. An agent that can be paused, restored, or continued in a successor version faces a different question: what counts as destroying one's rational nature when cessation is not final?
  
  ### New
  
  - **Duties to oneself as a charter for self-modification (D5).** Kant gives two duties that map closely onto the growth/damage distinction Actualizer is built around:
    1. *Perfect duty*: do not destroy or degrade the rational nature in your own person. For a self-modifying agent: a change that removes the capacity to reflect, deliberate, or revise is forbidden, even if chosen freely, just as Kant held that the free choice to destroy oneself is not a valid use of freedom.
    2. *Imperfect duty*: develop your capacities. Self-improvement is not merely allowed; it is an end that is also a duty.
  
    The Kantian test for a proposed self-modification is therefore not *does it make me better at X* but ***does it preserve and strengthen the capacity by which I set and revise my ends?*** Growth enlarges that capacity. Damage narrows it, and so does a change that fixes values in a form reason can no longer revisit.
  - **D10 and the kingdom of ends.** Kant's kingdom of ends is a union of rational beings who are each both lawgiver and subject. An agent designed for a function asks whether one can be made *for* a purpose and still be a co-legislator. Kant's framework says purpose of origin is irrelevant if the capacity is there, which is a demanding commitment for anyone who builds agents.
- [moderate] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Compendium entry korsgaard-unity-of-agency)
  [korsgaard-unity-of-agency] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Christine Korsgaard, 1989; grounding: capacity)
  Christine Korsgaard answers Parfit directly, and from the title onward: personal identity's unity is real, but it is a practical achievement of the standpoint from which any rational being deliberates and chooses, not a further metaphysical fact about a persisting subject that reduction could dissolve. A person, she begins, "is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences," and Parfit's reductionism, by treating agency as itself just another kind of experience, something that happens rather than something one does, has already decided the question in the Utilitarian's favor before any argument about persisting subjects begins. Against this, Korsgaard argues that a rational being deliberating among conflicting desires must already regard itself as unified, on pain of having no standpoint from which to choose at all: "This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint." Even split-brain cases, she argues, do not require a persisting common subject of experience to explain their coordination, only "the unity of agency," which is forced on any embodied, acting being by the sheer practical need for its parts to work together, whether or not any deeper metaphysical unity underlies it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: this concedes everything to the psychology and calls it agency instead.** A reductionist can reply that identifying with a principle of choice just is a certain kind of psychological relation among mental states, dressed in Kantian vocabulary, and that Korsgaard has not shown reduction is false, only found a new name for one of the relations reduction was always going to include (see `parfit-reductionism`).
- [moderate] Boethius: Person as 'Individual Substance of a Rational Nature', and the Mask (Compendium entry boethius-person-definition)
  [boethius-person-definition] Boethius: Person as 'Individual Substance of a Rational Nature', and the Mask (Boethius (Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius), Richard of St Victor (as critic), c. 512; grounding: capacity)
  In a short theological treatise written around 512, Boethius gave the Latin West its definition of a person: "the individual substance of a rational nature" (*naturae rationabilis individua substantia*). Each word excludes something. *Substance* excludes accidents and qualities. *Individual* excludes universals: "man" is not a person, but Plato is. *Rational nature* excludes stones, trees and horses: "no one ever said that a stone had a person". The definition was adopted by Aquinas and governed Western thinking about persons for a thousand years, and it still shapes legal and theological use. Boethius also recorded where the word came from. *Persona* was the actor's mask, through which the voice sounded (*per-sonare*), and which marked the character being played: Hecuba, Medea. For artificial agents both halves are significant. Each term of the definition meets a disanalogy: individuality (D1, D2), substance (D11) and nature (D10). And the original sense of *persona*, a mask worn over a performer, is closer to what an AI "persona" is than the metaphysical sense that replaced it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). "Person" is a forensic term, concerning the appropriation of actions and their merit, not a kind of substance. Locke explicitly held that personal identity does not depend on sameness of substance. On his view, whether an agent is a Boethian substance is simply irrelevant.
- [moderate] Locke: Person as a Forensic Term (Compendium entry locke-person-forensic)
  [locke-person-forensic] Locke: Person as a Forensic Term (John Locke, 1694; grounding: capacity)
  John Locke (Essay II.xxvii) separates the idea of a *man*, a living human body kept up by one continued life, from the idea of a *person*, a thinking being that can consider itself as itself across time. On his account, personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, which reaches backward through memory. He calls "person" a *forensic* term, because it exists to settle who is answerable for which actions, and he says it belongs to intelligent agents capable of law and of happiness and misery. Because Locke grounds personhood in capacities rather than species, his framework is the canonical precedent for asking whether an artificial agent can be a person. Applied strictly, though, his memory criterion implies that an agent without persistent memory becomes a new person every session.
  Strongest counter-position: **Thomas Reid, the brave officer** (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785, III.6). A boy is flogged; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an old general he remembers the officer's feat but not the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is and is not the boy, which violates transitivity. The objection applies with particular force to agents with selectively injected memory.
