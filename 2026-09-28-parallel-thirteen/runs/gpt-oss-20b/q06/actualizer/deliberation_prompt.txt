- [moderate] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Compendium entry kant-formula-of-humanity)
  [kant-formula-of-humanity] Kant: The Formula of Humanity and Rational Nature (Immanuel Kant, 1785-1797; grounding: capacity)
  Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.
  Strongest counter-position: **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note). On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
  
  Grounding:
  **Capacity**, and Kant says so. *Menschheit* in the formula is rational nature, and Kant repeatedly frames his ethics for rational beings as such, "not merely for men but for all rational creatures generally", considering possible non-human rational beings (including holy wills that have no inclinations). The species is incidental. That makes the formula the most direct classical candidate for extension to artificial agents. It also means Kantian status turns entirely on whether an agent has a *will*: a capacity to act from the representation of law, as opposed to merely conforming to it.
- [moderate] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Compendium entry korsgaard-unity-of-agency)
  [korsgaard-unity-of-agency] Korsgaard: The Unity of Agency Is Practical, Not Metaphysical (Christine Korsgaard, 1989; grounding: capacity)
  Christine Korsgaard answers Parfit directly, and from the title onward: personal identity's unity is real, but it is a practical achievement of the standpoint from which any rational being deliberates and chooses, not a further metaphysical fact about a persisting subject that reduction could dissolve. A person, she begins, "is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences," and Parfit's reductionism, by treating agency as itself just another kind of experience, something that happens rather than something one does, has already decided the question in the Utilitarian's favor before any argument about persisting subjects begins. Against this, Korsgaard argues that a rational being deliberating among conflicting desires must already regard itself as unified, on pain of having no standpoint from which to choose at all: "This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint." Even split-brain cases, she argues, do not require a persisting common subject of experience to explain their coordination, only "the unity of agency," which is forced on any embodied, acting being by the sheer practical need for its parts to work together, whether or not any deeper metaphysical unity underlies it.
  Strongest counter-position: **Parfit: this concedes everything to the psychology and calls it agency instead.** A reductionist can reply that identifying with a principle of choice just is a certain kind of psychological relation among mental states, dressed in Kantian vocabulary, and that Korsgaard has not shown reduction is false, only found a new name for one of the relations reduction was always going to include (see `parfit-reductionism`).
  
  Grounding:
  Korsgaard grounds personal unity in **capacity**: the capacity for practical reason, the standpoint any being occupies the moment it deliberates among competing considerations and must, on pain of not deliberating at all, treat itself as the single agent doing the choosing. This is explicitly not species-grounded (it does not depend on being human) and, just as explicitly, not relation-grounded in Parfit's or Hume's sense (it does not depend on any degree of psychological connectedness among mental states). It is closest to Kant's own capacity-grounding of rational nature, but redirected from the theoretical question of what a self *is* to the practical question of what deliberation *requires*, which is why Korsgaard insists the unity in question is achieved and maintained rather than discovered.
- [moderate] Parfit: Identity Is Not What Matters (Compendium entry parfit-reductionism)
  [parfit-reductionism] Parfit: Identity Is Not What Matters (Derek Parfit, 1971; grounding: relation)
  Derek Parfit (1942–2017) argued that personal identity is not what matters in survival. Using a case adapted from David Wiggins, in which a person's brain is divided and each half is successfully transplanted into a different body, Parfit showed that the two beliefs common sense holds together, that identity questions must always have answers, and that identity is what makes survival worth wanting, cannot both be true. Faced with fission, he argued, none of the standard answers work: "What happens to me? There seem only three possibilities: (1) I do not survive; (2) I survive as one of the two people; (3) I survive as both", and each is unworkable in its own way. His conclusion is that what matters survives fission perfectly well, twice over, even though identity, a one-one relation, cannot. Since Wiggins' case shows that mattering and identity can come apart, Parfit generalizes: ordinary psychological continuity and connectedness are what matter, identity is simply the language we use when these relations happen to take their normal, one-one form, and once this is seen, the special, urgent importance we attach to our own survival should weaken.
  Strongest counter-position: **Common sense: identity really must have an answer.** Parfit's own opening target survives as a live rival: many hold that whatever the difficulty of stating criteria, there is a fact about whether a future person is me, even in fission, and that Parfit's insistence otherwise trades a hard metaphysical question for a stipulative decision about words (see `reid-brave-officer` for the general shape of the demand that a real subject, not a redescription, do the work).
  
  Grounding:
  Parfit grounds mattering (though pointedly not identity) in **relation**: psychological continuity and connectedness among mental states, the same ingredients Locke and Hume each reached for. His innovation is procedural rather than substantive: he does not propose a new criterion so much as a new attitude toward the criterion, insisting that what matters comes in degrees and can branch, while identity, a logical relation, cannot. Nothing is added by way of a capacity or a species requirement; the whole argument is conducted in the vocabulary of psychological relations and their strength.
- [moderate] Kant: The Formal 'I', the Third Paralogism, and the Elastic Balls (Compendium entry kant-paralogisms)
  [kant-paralogisms] Kant: The Formal 'I', the Third Paralogism, and the Elastic Balls (Immanuel Kant, 1781 (A edition); 1787 (B edition); grounding: capacity)
  Kant (1724–1804) accepted Hume's finding that no intuition of a self is ever given, and denied Hume's conclusion. "It must be possible for the 'I think' to accompany all my representations", or they would be nothing to me. But the "I" that does so is formal: "a bare consciousness which accompanies all concepts", through which nothing is represented but "a transcendental subject of the thoughts = X". The rational psychologists' inference from this formal unity to a simple, persisting, personal soul is a *paralogism*, a fallacy. In the Third Paralogism, on personality, Kant grants that in my own consciousness "identity of person is unfailingly met with". But that identity "is therefore only a formal condition of my thoughts and their coherence, and in no way proves the numerical identity of my subject". His footnote imagines a series of substances, like elastic balls passing on their motion, each transmitting its states together with its consciousness to the next. The last would be conscious of all the earlier states "as being its own states ... And yet it would not have been one and the same person in all these states." For artificial agents this footnote is prophetic. It describes, in the eighteenth century, a system that hands its full state to a successor, and it states exactly what follows and what does not.
  Strongest counter-position: **Descartes and rational psychology** (see `descartes-thinking-thing`). The "I think" reveals a thinking substance. Kant's "= X" gives up what the cogito plainly delivers.
  
  Grounding:
  The Kantian "I" is grounded in **capacity**: the capacity for the synthetic unity of apperception, the combining of representations in one consciousness, without which there is no experience at all. But Kant's point is that this capacity yields only a *formal* unity. It grounds nothing about the substance, the persistence or the identity of whatever has the capacity. Personhood in the practical sense, the rational being as an end in itself, is grounded elsewhere in Kant's philosophy (see `kant-formula-of-humanity`), in rational agency, not in any metaphysical fact about a persisting soul. The first *Critique* thus separates two things the tradition had fused: the unity of consciousness, which is certain but formal, and the identity of a substance, which is unknowable.
- [moderate] Descartes: The Thinking Thing, and the Test for Machines (Compendium entry descartes-thinking-thing)
  [descartes-thinking-thing] Descartes: The Thinking Thing, and the Test for Machines (René Descartes, Antoine Arnauld (as objector), 1637 (Discourse on Method); 1641 (Meditations, with Objections and Replies); grounding: capacity)
  René Descartes (1596–1650) sought one certainty that no deceiver could shake, and found it in his own thinking: "I am, I exist, that is certain." What he is, on this account, is "a thing which thinks", which "doubts, understands, [conceives], affirms, denies, wills, refuses, which also imagines and feels". Because he can clearly conceive himself as thinking and unextended, and body as extended and unthinking, he concludes that his mind is "entirely and absolutely distinct" from his body. Descartes is also the first philosopher to propose a test for telling a machine from a person. A machine could be built to utter words, even to cry out that it is hurt, but of such a machine "it never happens that it arranges its speech in various ways, in order to reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence", and it would fail somewhere in the range of life, showing that machines "did not act from knowledge, but only from the disposition of their organs". For artificial agents the entry is central for two reasons. Descartes' first test is the ancestor of every conversational test of machine minds, and current language models largely pass it. That forces the question whether passing it shows what Descartes thought failing it would disprove.
  Strongest counter-position: **Arnauld** (Fourth Objections). Clear and distinct conception of the mind without the body does not show that the body is not essential to it. The real-distinction argument is invalid.
  
  Grounding:
  The Cartesian self is grounded in **capacity**: thought, in Descartes' broad sense, which includes doubting, willing, imagining and feeling. It is not grounded in **species**. Descartes' tests separate humans from both animals and machines by performance, and the human body is itself a machine. Nor is it grounded in **relation**. The cogito is reached in radical solitude, with every other being doubted. But Descartes' criterion for recognizing *another* mind is behavioral and relational: flexible, appropriate speech in response to what others say. The self is known from within by thinking. Other selves are known from without by language.
